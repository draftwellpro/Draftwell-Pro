Austen Wrote 145-Word Sentences. Here's Why They Still Work

Long sentences are not the enemy of readability. Jane Austen shows that rhythm, variation, and structure matter far more than raw length.

August 1, 2026 · Draftwell Pro Blog

If writing advice on the internet has a favorite rule, it is this: keep your sentences short. The implication is that long sentences are automatically difficult. Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice argues otherwise.

Draftwell Pro's analysis found an average sentence length of 17.8 words, but the novel also contains a remarkable 145-word sentence. Even more interesting, 16.4% of all sentences exceed 30 words. Yet the book still earns a 64.5 Flesch Reading Ease score, placing it comfortably in the standard readability range.

Those numbers are not contradictory. They reveal something many writers overlook: readability depends far more on rhythm and organization than on sentence length alone.

The surprising part The famous 145-word sentence is an exception, not Austen's default style. The novel works because long sentences appear inside a broader pattern of variation.

One long sentence tells you almost nothing

Writers often fixate on their longest sentence, but that number has little meaning by itself. A manuscript with one 150-word sentence is completely different from one where every sentence approaches that length.

That is why Draftwell Pro measures both extremes: the longest sentence and the percentage of sentences exceeding 30 words. Together, those metrics reveal whether complexity is occasional, intentional, or overwhelming.

The chapter breakdown shows another interesting pattern: Austen does not distribute long sentences evenly. Some chapters contain bursts of complexity while others rely on shorter rhythms. Sentence length changes with the needs of the scene.

A long sentence is interesting. A pattern of long sentences is a style.

Variation is what creates rhythm

Austen's prose works because it refuses to settle into a predictable cadence. The average sentence length is only one part of the story. The more revealing number is the 15.3-word standard deviation, which shows substantial movement between short and long sentences.

Short sentences create emphasis. Medium sentences carry the narrative. Longer sentences allow observations and emotional shifts to unfold naturally. Together they create a rhythm readers feel without consciously measuring.

The problem is not length. The problem is losing the reader before the period arrives.

Why readability survives long sentences

If long sentences automatically made prose difficult, Austen's readability score would be far lower. Instead, the novel remains accessible because the underlying structure stays clear.

Readers can follow complex thoughts when clauses build logically and ideas arrive in a deliberate order. Confusion usually comes from disorganized syntax, not sentence length.

What this means for your own manuscript

  • Do not judge your prose by its longest sentence alone.
  • Look at how frequently long sentences appear.
  • Study where those sentences occur in your chapters.
  • Use sentence variation to create rhythm.
  • Fix structure before automatically shortening.

Great prose is not built from short sentences or long sentences. It is built from sentences working together.
