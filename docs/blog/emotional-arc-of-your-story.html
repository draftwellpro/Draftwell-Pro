What Emotional Arc Does Your Story Hold?

Six basic shapes explain most stories ever told. Here's how to find out which one you're writing — and why a certain bowl of porridge turned out to be a tragedy.

July 31, 2026 · Draftwell Pro Blog

Every story you love has a shape. Not a plot — a shape. If you plotted how good or bad things feel for the protagonist, scene by scene, from page one to the last line, the line you'd draw would trace something. A climb. A fall. A dip and a recovery. In 2016, researchers Andrew Reagan, Lewis Mitchell, Dilan Kiley, Christopher Danforth, and Peter Dodds ran sentiment analysis across roughly 1,700 works of fiction and found that nearly all of them collapse into just six recurring emotional shapes.

The six shapes

Each one has a folk name, because each one is a story you already know:

Rags to Riches
steady rise
Tragedy
steady fall
Man in a Hole
fall, then rise
Icarus
rise, then fall
Cinderella
rise, fall, rise
Oedipus
fall, rise, fall

The finding that made writers sit up wasn't just that six shapes exist — it's that some combinations correlate with commercial success far more than others. In Reagan et al.'s download data, Man in a Hole style arcs (or an Icarus-then-a-Man-in-a-Hole double dip) were disproportionately common among the most-downloaded titles. Readers, it turns out, like watching someone fall into a hole and climb back out. A lot.

Turning that into something a writer can actually use

Knowing the theory is one thing. Knowing which shape your manuscript actually makes is another, and that's the part most writers never get to check, because doing it by hand means re-reading your whole book with a highlighter and a mood ring.

So that's what I built into Draftwell Pro. Under the hood it's a hand-assembled valence lexicon — words scored from mildly positive to strongly positive, and the mirror on the negative side — run against every scene in your manuscript. Each scene gets averaged into a tone score, and then all of those scores get centered on your book's own baseline, so "0" always means "typical for this book," not some universal neutral. Chain those centered scores together in reading order, smooth the line, and the shape falls out on its own. From there the app pattern-matches it against the six Reagan shapes and tells you which one you're closest to.

Case study: a bowl of porridge, and a tragedy

To stress-test the feature, I ran it against something everyone already knows the "plot" of: Goldilocks and the Three Bears. Nine scenes, three chapters, 2,133 words. A cozy nursery classic if there ever was one.

The app called it a Tragedy. (Want to see every metric behind that call? Here's the full manuscript report this post is drawn from.)

Read the chart the way you'd read your own: the opening stays warm through the bear family's morning routine and Goldilocks wandering into the woods — bowls of porridge, a cozy cottage, curiosity, nothing dark yet. Then, right around "The Chairs," the line turns and it does not turn back. By "Someone's Been Here" and "The Escape," the tone has dropped hard and stays there. No third-act bounce. That's the mechanical definition of Tragedy: a steady fall with no recovery, as opposed to Man in a Hole, which is that exact same fall followed by a climb back out.

The book's middle act — "Goldilocks Arrives," the chairs, the porridge, the beds — eats up 45% of the total word count, the classic saggy middle. And every single chapter, including the last, closes on a soft ending: no open question, no unresolved line, nothing that pulls the reader forward. The closing line of the whole book is Goldilocks lying awake thinking about Baby Bear's broken chair — genuinely a quiet, unresolved note to end on. Structurally, there was never a beat left for the arc to climb back out of its hole. That's not a flaw in this particular telling — fairy tales often end on a moral aftertaste rather than a triumphant high — but it's exactly the kind of thing this analysis is built to surface without you having to intuit it.

Why this is worth checking on your own manuscript

Here's the practical takeaway, and it isn't "always write a Man in a Hole." It's this: know what you're writing on purpose. If your beta readers keep saying your ending feels flat, or that the book "just kind of stops" rather than resolves, an emotional-arc chart will often show you why before you can articulate it yourself — a long fall with nothing to answer it. If you're deliberately writing a Tragedy or an Oedipus arc, the tool confirms you nailed the shape instead of accidentally landing there. And if you thought you'd written an uplifting Cinderella arc and the chart says Icarus, that's worth a second look at your last chapter.

A flat or mismatched arc doesn't mean the writing is bad — it means the shape and the intent haven't been checked against each other yet.

A couple of honest caveats, because a tool like this is only as good as its limits: it reads word choice, scene by scene, against a general-purpose valence lexicon — it doesn't understand irony, negation, or a scene where "everything is fine" is dripping with dread. A short manuscript (like our 2,133-word bear family) also gives the algorithm less to average over than a 90,000-word novel would, so treat the shape as a strong hint on a short text and a confident read on a full-length one. Use it the way you'd use a beta reader's gut reaction: as a signal to investigate, not a verdict to obey.

Try it on your own draft

If you've never plotted your own book's emotional arc, it's worth ten minutes — you may find you already know exactly where the flat stretch is, or you may be surprised by where the story actually turns.
