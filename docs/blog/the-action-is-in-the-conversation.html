The Action Is in the Conversation

Sometimes the fastest-moving scenes are the ones where nobody moves at all.

August 3, 2026 · Draftwell Pro Blog

When writers think about pacing, they often think about movement. Someone runs. Someone fights. Someone escapes. But Draftwell Pro's analysis of Pride and Prejudice reveals something surprising. The novel contains only 1.5 action verbs per 1,000 words, earning a remarkably low Vitality score of 0.7/10. By that measure alone, you might expect a slow, static novel.

Instead, the book achieves a healthy Pacing Index of 5.2/10. The story keeps moving despite very little physical action. The explanation is simple: the momentum comes from conversation.

The interesting part isn't any single number. Together these metrics tell a story. Austen uses very little physical action, relatively long scenes, and nearly half her words are dialogue—yet the novel still maintains steady forward momentum. The action isn't happening in the characters' bodies. It's happening in their relationships.

Physical movement isn't narrative movement

This is one of the easiest mistakes for writers to make. We often assume a scene feels slow because characters are sitting in a room talking. But readers rarely care whether characters are moving. They care whether the situation is changing.

Every important conversation in Pride and Prejudice shifts something. Elizabeth gains information. Darcy reveals vulnerability. A proposal changes the future. A misunderstanding grows worse. Social status rises or falls. Every exchange leaves the characters in a different emotional position than where they started.

That is action.

Momentum isn't created by movement. It's created by change.

Why the pacing still works

The surrounding metrics reinforce the same conclusion. Nearly 47% of the novel consists of dialogue, giving readers a constant stream of interaction. At the same time, 61.9% of paragraphs are 50 words or fewer, creating plenty of visual breaks that keep pages turning.

Perhaps most interesting, the novel's scene density is only 0.50 scenes per thousand words. Austen is not rapidly jumping between locations or constantly cutting away to new scenes. Individual scenes are relatively long. Instead of changing locations to create momentum, she changes relationships inside the scene.

The result is a novel that feels lively despite containing remarkably little physical action.

What Draftwell Pro helps you see

This is exactly the kind of pattern that's difficult to spot while reading your own manuscript. A chapter may feel slow, but why? Is there too little dialogue? Are scenes too long? Are conversations failing to change anything? Or are you simply lacking physical action?

Draftwell Pro separates these signals instead of lumping them together. It measures dialogue ratio, pacing, vitality, action verb density, paragraph rhythm, scene density, and dozens of other metrics independently so you can understand why a chapter feels the way it does.

That distinction matters because the solution depends on the cause. If your pacing is weak because conversations never change the situation, adding another chase scene won't fix the underlying problem.

What this means for your own writing

  • Make every conversation change something.
  • Give dialogue emotional or social consequences.
  • Shift power between characters during a scene.
  • End conversations differently than they began.
  • Don't confuse movement with momentum.

Try this on your own manuscript

Open one of your dialogue-heavy chapters in Draftwell Pro. Look beyond the word count. Compare its Dialogue Ratio, Vitality, Pacing Index, paragraph structure, and scene density.

If the pacing feels slow despite healthy dialogue, the issue may not be the conversation itself. It may be that nothing important changes during it.

The most valuable insights rarely come from a single metric. They come from seeing how multiple metrics explain each other.

Austen reminds us that readers don't keep turning pages because characters keep moving. They keep turning pages because the story keeps changing.
